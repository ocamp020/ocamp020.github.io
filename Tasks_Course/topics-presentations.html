    Occupational tasks and AI exposure

    Which parts of an occupation could AI perform, and how does that relate to careers in consulting, finance, management, and sales? This presentation introduces O*NET, connects economics and business programs to occupations, and compares measures of potential exposure with observed AI use.

    References

    • Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock (2024), GPTs are GPTs: Labor Market Impact Potential of LLMs.
    • Felten, Raj & Seamans (2021), Occupational Exposure to Artificial Intelligence: A Novel Measure and Its Implications.
    • Massenkoff & McCrory (2026), Labor Market Impacts of AI: A New Measure and Early Evidence.

    Data resources: O*NET occupational data; TaskNet: worker-based task inventories (appendix).

    Who uses AI, and for what?

    How widely is generative AI used, which workers adopt it, and what do they use it for? Survey evidence and ChatGPT conversation data show how adoption varies across occupations and tasks. The presentation distinguishes exposure, actual use, reported time savings, and measured productivity.

    References

    • Bick, Blandin, Deming & Schumacher (2026), What Work Does Generative AI Do?.
    • Bick, Blandin & Deming (2026), The Rapid Adoption of Generative AI.
    • Chatterji et al. (2025), How People Use ChatGPT.

    Expertise and the productivity effects of AI

    Who gains when AI changes the expertise needed to do a job? A task model and historical occupation evidence lead into a customer-support rollout and a randomized patent-lawyer experiment. The results distinguish faster or better assisted work from the development of independent judgment.

    References

    • Autor & Thompson (2025), Expertise.
    • Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2025), Generative AI at Work.
    • Autor et al. (2026), Does AI Assistance Enhance or Erode Expertise? Evidence from a Three-Month Field Experiment in Patent Drafting.

    What do we know about robot automation?

    How does robot automation affect firms, workers, and local labour markets? U.S., Danish, and Brazilian evidence connects exposure-based designs, firms’ adoption choices, and the distinction between robots and worker-operated tools. The presentation explains Bartik instruments, separates relative local estimates from model-based aggregate effects, and closes with evidence on productivity and worker adjustment.

    References

    • Acemoglu & Restrepo (2020), Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets.
    • Humlum (2021), Robot Adoption and Labor Market Dynamics (November 28 working paper).
    • de Souza & Li (2025), Robots, Tools, and Jobs: Evidence from Brazilian Labor Markets.
    • Graetz & Michaels (2018), Robots at Work (comparison and appendix).

    Data resources: Bartik / shift-share instruments: Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel (2025); The missing intercept problem: Benjamin Moll.
